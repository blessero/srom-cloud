# How MB illustrates a SROM article

Read before every brief. The principles are distilled from MB's own cases (§ Cases); each case adds to them. They
describe tendencies, not a formula: MB's angle on an article is his, and a brief offers him options along these lines,
plus one or two he would not have thought of.

## Principles

1. **Images are part of the reading.** About 8 for an average-length article, more for long ones; about 4 when they
   supplement what the author supplied. Each one earns its place by what it shows about the text.
2. **Immersion is welcome.** Scholarly as SROM is, a justified emotional factor makes history present: add striking,
   dramatic images (people in action, the scene, the protagonists, perpetrators too) to the factual but often visually
   flat material. Where a text is short on substance, they can carry more of the page (Gontarek), but that is the
   exception, not the model.
3. **Licentia poetica, truthful caption.** An image from another moment or a neighbouring place is acceptable when it
   shows the thing more strongly: the Admiralicja damaged in 1939 with German soldiers beside it, for the fighting of
   1944; insurgents in Śródmieście, for Ochota. The caption always states the real date and place (Kanon § 10.1);
   never caption an image as what it is not.
4. **Show the source itself.** When the text names a document, law, periodical, register, print or court record,
   show it: the *Congressional Globe* of 30 May 1866, the *Code noir*, a passenger list to the colonies, an
   interrogation record. Mine the footnotes for these first.
5. **Abstract ideas, documentary images.** In theoretical papers, or papers with important theory, an abstract idea
   can occasionally be depicted, but only through an image with documentary value of its own: the real archive of a
   celebrated institution (NYPL) for Ostendorf's *u-nieobecnianie*, the silence of archives and historians; never a
   generic stock-like shot (a random library).
6. **Documentary over cliché; clichés only critically.** Prefer documentary and street photography and unexpected
   modern settings (Rizzuto's Romani woman begging on the Bowery, 1950) to picturesque filler: no dancing "Gypsy" as eye
   candy. But many papers deal with depictions and stereotypes, so stereotypical images belong where they are the object
   of analysis, set in a critical comparison and captioned as such: Ostendorf p. 35 (La Tour, Hals, Rousseau: no fixed
   "Gypsy colour"); Fotta p. 78 (Sorolla's folklorism for the American market, Matisse's exoticisation in the service
   of form, Timóteo da Costa's individualised portrait restoring the sitter's subjecthood, Corot's 19th-century topos).
7. **Immersive spreads.** A period view of the place, even one that documents no event, puts the reader there; prefer
   the rare or only contemporary view (Lassus, *Veüe et Perspective de la Nouvelle Orleans*, 1726, the one view of
   the city under French rule). Good for a double page.
8. **Extensions toward the volume's axis.** Sometimes MB adds material beyond the text, along lines the author implies
   here or in related work, or that the volume's theme calls for (vol. 18 → 19: racialisation). In Ostendorf: a
   *pintura de castas* (Clapera, *lobo* of an Indian and a *torna atrás*), an abolitionist photograph of "white"
   children born into slavery (1863), Laughlin's skin-pigmentation chart of Jamaicans for the Eugenics Record Office,
   the Nuremberg Laws' chart of *Mischlinge*, Hosemann's 1828 primer setting a *Zigeunerin* beside a half-naked
   African, details of paintings showing how differently artists coloured Roma (La Tour, Hals, Rousseau). Few,
   grouped on one page or spread, with long analytic captions. Propose them as a separate, marked group; never as
   the bulk of a brief.
9. **Compositions.** Pairs and sets that are read together on one page (*Po lewej / Po prawej / Od lewej*); details
   cut from paintings; a map cut from a larger sheet. Look for sets that compare (the same subject by different
   hands, the same classification in two empires).
10. **The caption carries the argument, the register stays dry** (Kanon § 10.2). An analytic caption names the
    mechanism (*blanqueamiento*, the fractions of "black blood") in bare facts, without outrage.
11. **Provenance upward.** Prefer the original holder's copy and credit it; from an aggregator or a user upload, trace
    the original (sources.md § Tracing). When nothing better exists (a fotopolska.eu upload whose uploader does not
    know the author and makes no claim), record what was done and leave the decision to MB (rights.md, class E).
12. **Time beats the better image.** A stronger image that needs permission lost to a free one when time was short
    (Kamiński on trial, rights with IPN). So: flag permission-needed candidates at the brief stage, with the holder
    and the likely lead time, and give each slot a free fallback.

## Cases

### Gontarek, „Reduta Wawelska” 1944 r. (vol. 18, pp. 139–157)
Author supplied nothing; the Romani thread barely present. Aim: an immersive, dramatic experience to compensate
(principle 2). Images: a map of Warsaw cut from a larger sheet (probably David Rumsey, p. 142); the Admiralicja as the
focal point, including the 1939 photograph with damage and German soldiers (pp. 156–157, fotopolska.eu; uploader asked,
author unknown, no claim); the surrounding Ochota buildings; SS-Brigadeführer Bronisław Kamiński as a protagonist
(NAC; a stronger image of him on trial was IPN's, no time to ask); Waffen-Sturmbrigade RONA (Commons); striking
insurgent shots, some from Śródmieście (principle 3). Sources used: Wikimedia Commons (pp. 139, 142, 143, 145), NAC
(p. 140 and many others), Polona (p. 145), David Rumsey (p. 142), fotopolska.eu (pp. 156–157).

### Ostendorf, Historia Romów amerykańskich (vol. 18)
Author suggested many images; many used, many more sourced. Most are historical sources tied to the text
(principle 4): the *Congressional Globe*, 30 May 1866 (HathiTrust); the *Code noir* (ANOM, Colonies AC) and its 1742
edition (Musée d'histoire de Nantes, via Commons; the code is not in the text: context toward the race theme); passenger
lists from France to the colonies 1716–1830 (ANOM F5B 44, Dunkirk, *Le Tilleul*, 20–21 May 1720); the French Superior
Council's interrogation of Jean Chevallier dit Lachaum (Louisiana Colonial Judicial Records). An archive view from the
New York Public Library for the argument about the production of history (principle 5). Rizzuto's Bowery photograph,
1950, Library of Congress (principle 6). Lassus's view of New Orleans, 1726, ANOM DFC Louisiane 71-6A, as an immersive
spread (principle 7). Pages 32 and 35: the race extension (principle 8) — Clapera's *pintura de castas* (Denver Art
Museum, photo Steven Zucker, Flickr); *Rosa, Charley, Rebecca* (Charles Paxson, 1863, Library of Congress); Laughlin's
pigmentation chart (Harry H. Laughlin Papers, Truman State University, Lantern Slides, Brown Box 807); the Nuremberg
chart (Commons); Hosemann, *Das allegrösste Bilder ABC* (title as MB gave it; check before reuse), 1828 (Library of Congress); La Tour (Met), Hals (Louvre),
Rousseau (Met). Similar extensions in Fotta and Takács, vol. 18.
